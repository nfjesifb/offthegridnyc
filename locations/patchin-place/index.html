Background

From the original map

Patchin Place, a tiny gated cul-de-sac off West 10th Street in Greenwich Village, New York City, was developed in 1848–1849 by Aaron Patchin, a surveyor who built ten three-story brick row houses on land he acquired from his father-in-law, Samuel Milligan. Originally designed as modest housing—possibly for workers at the nearby Brevoort House hotel—the homes featured small dormer windows and shared a courtyard with a gas lamp, still lit today. Early residents were largely working-class, including Basque immigrants who ran boarding houses there in the late 19th century. By the early 20th century, Patchin Place transformed into a bohemian enclave, attracting literary and artistic figures like E.E. Cummings, who lived at No. 4 from 1923 until his death in 1962, and Djuna Barnes, a resident of No. 5 for over 40 years. Theodore Dreiser and John Reed also spent time there, cementing its reputation as a creative haven. Rumors persist that the houses were built with salvaged bricks from the 1832 demolition of Newgate Prison, though this lacks firm evidence. Part of the Greenwich Village Historic District since 1969, Patchin Place remains a quiet, historic retreat, its charm preserved amid the bustling city.
